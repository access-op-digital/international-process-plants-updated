## Planetary Mixers vs Planetary Dispersers

Both designs move their tools in a planetary orbit, and the high-speed disperser blade separates them.

| Factor | Planetary Mixer | Planetary Disperser |
|-----|-----|-----|
| Agitators | Blades that turn on their own axes while orbiting the vessel, two of them on a double planetary design | A slow planetary stirrer plus a high-speed saw-tooth dispersion blade |
| Action | Kneading and compression in thick pastes | High shear at the disperser disc, with the planetary sweep feeding fresh material to it |
| Viscosity | Double planetary designs handle 6 to 8 million cP | Typically 100,000 cP to 2 million cP |
| In IPP's Inventory | Ross HDM 150 in titanium | Ross PD-2 PowerMix |

**Q: How does a planetary mixer work?**
A: A planetary mixer turns its vertical agitator shaft on its own axis while the shaft orbits the inside of a stationary vessel, much as a planet circles the sun. The blades reach every part of the bowl on each orbit, so the batch blends without unmixed pockets.

**Q: What is a change can or pony mixer?**
A: A change can mixer works its batch in a removable can that rolls or lifts away when the batch is done, so the next can takes its place. On a pony mixer, vertical blades turn inside a can that rotates in the opposite direction while a scraper returns material from the wall; the Drais FH80 in stock follows the change-can layout, its lower chamber riding on casters and flanging hydraulically to the upper chamber.

**Q: Which mixers run under vacuum or pressure?**
A: The Ross PD-2 PowerMix runs under vacuum in a jacketed can, the Drais FH80 chamber is rated 2.4 bar (34.8 psi) and full vacuum with a jacket rated 4.5 bar (65.3 psi), and the Haagen-Rinau Unimax body is rated 0.69 bar (10 psi) at 150 °C (302 °F) with a jacket rated 6.1 bar (88 psi). The titanium Ross HDM 150 planetary mixer works at atmospheric pressure without a jacket.
