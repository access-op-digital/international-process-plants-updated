## Frequently Asked Questions About Shaft & Mixer Agitators

Answers to common questions about used shaft and mixer agitators in exotic alloys, covering materials, specifications, condition, and purchasing.

**Q: What is Hastelloy C-276 and why is it used for agitator shafts?**
A: Hastelloy C-276 is a nickel-molybdenum-chromium superalloy (UNS N10276) that resists pitting, crevice corrosion, and stress corrosion cracking in both oxidizing and reducing environments. Hastelloy C-276 agitator shafts are specified for reactors handling hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), chlorinated solvents, acetic acid, formic acid, and wet chlorine gas — environments where stainless steel 316 corrodes rapidly. The alloy withstands these chemicals at elevated temperatures without losing mechanical strength. IPP stocks Hastelloy C-276 shaft agitators, the majority unused, with PMI verification and MTR documentation on every unit.

**Q: When should I choose a titanium agitator shaft over Hastelloy?**
A: You should choose a titanium agitator shaft when your process involves chloride-rich environments (seawater, brine, chloride solutions), oxidizing acids (nitric acid, chromic acid), or applications where the highest strength-to-weight ratio matters. Titanium resists pitting in chloride media at temperatures up to 260°C (500°F) — conditions that can challenge even Hastelloy C-276. Hastelloy C-276 is the better choice for reducing acids (HCl, H2SO4) and chlorinated solvents. In practice, the decision comes down to your specific process chemistry: if your vessel handles seawater or high-chloride media, titanium excels; if it handles reducing acids or mixed acid environments, Hastelloy C-276 is the standard. Contact IPP to discuss which alloy best fits your application.

**Q: What chemicals can Hastelloy C-276 agitator shafts resist?**
A: Hastelloy C-276 agitator shafts resist a wide range of aggressive chemicals including: hydrochloric acid (all concentrations, including hot), sulfuric acid (up to approximately 70% concentration), chlorinated solvents (methylene chloride, chloroform, carbon tetrachloride), acetic acid and formic acid, wet chlorine gas, hypochlorite solutions (bleach), phosphoric acid, and mixed acid environments. The alloy also withstands ferric and cupric chloride solutions that cause rapid pitting in stainless steel. Hastelloy C-276 is one of the few alloys that resists both oxidizing and reducing conditions, making it the default choice for reactors where process chemistry varies between batches.

**Q: What shaft diameters does IPP stock?**
A: IPP stocks shaft and mixer agitators in three shaft diameters: 63.5 mm (2.5 in) — the most common size in inventory, available in Hastelloy C-276 from Lightnin; 82.6 mm (3.3 in) — available in Hastelloy C-276 for longer shaft lengths up to 4,050 mm (159.4 in); and 88.9 mm (3.5 in) — available in titanium. Shaft diameters are measured at the bearing journal and are matched to specific drive coupling dimensions. Provide your existing shaft diameter and IPP will confirm compatibility with available inventory.

**Q: What impeller types are available?**
A: IPP's shaft and mixer agitators include pitch blade impellers — the most common configuration in stock. Pitch blade impellers generate combined axial and radial flow, making them effective for blending, suspension, and heat transfer applications. Where specified, impellers feature 4-blade configurations with blade spans of 330.2 mm (13 in). Pitch blade impellers are standard on Lightnin agitator assemblies. If your application requires a different impeller type (turbine, hydrofoil, or anchor), contact IPP to discuss custom impeller fitting on available shafts.

**Q: How do I measure my existing agitator shaft for a replacement?**
A: To measure your existing agitator shaft for a replacement, you need five key dimensions: shaft diameter at the bearing journal (where the shaft seats in the seal or bearing housing), overall shaft length from coupling end to impeller tip, coupling type and dimensions (keyway, spline, or taper) at the drive end, impeller attachment method and location along the shaft, and impeller diameter (blade span). Also note the material of construction from the nameplate or MTR. If the shaft is damaged and you cannot measure it, provide the agitator drive manufacturer and model — IPP can determine the correct shaft dimensions from OEM specifications. Dimensional drawings and photographs of your existing shaft help IPP match the correct replacement from inventory.

**Q: Are IPP's shaft/mixer agitators used or new?**
A: IPP stocks both used and unused shaft/mixer agitators. The majority are unused — meaning they were manufactured but never installed in a process vessel. The remaining units are in used condition with documented service history. Unused exotic alloy shafts are exceptionally rare on the secondary market because Hastelloy C-276 and titanium components are almost always built to order for specific projects. IPP acquired these shafts from plant closures and cancelled projects, making them available at a fraction of new procurement cost.

**Q: What does "unused" mean for an agitator shaft?**
A: "Unused" means the agitator shaft was manufactured — forged, machined, and fitted with impellers — but was never installed in a reactor vessel or exposed to process media. The shaft has zero operating hours and no corrosion history. Unused shafts are typically surplus from new plant construction projects that were cancelled, delayed, or down-scoped. They represent new-condition metallurgy and mechanical properties at used-equipment pricing. IPP verifies unused condition through visual inspection (no corrosion, no wear marks, no process residue), PMI testing, and original documentation review.

**Q: How is shaft straightness verified?**
A: IPP verifies shaft straightness by placing the shaft on V-blocks or in a lathe and measuring total indicated runout (TIR) with a dial indicator at multiple points along the shaft length. A straight agitator shaft should have a TIR within the OEM's specified runout tolerance — typically 0.05 to 0.10 mm (0 in) (0.002 to 0.004 in) per meter of shaft length. Excessive runout causes vibration, premature mechanical seal failure, and accelerated bearing wear. IPP includes the measured runout values in the shaft inspection report. If a shaft exceeds acceptable tolerance, it is flagged and not offered for sale unless it can be straightened.

**Q: What is the difference between a shaft/mixer agitator and a drive agitator?**
A: A shaft/mixer agitator is the shaft assembly — the rotating shaft with impellers and blades that extends into the reactor vessel and does the actual mixing. A drive agitator is the motor, gearbox, and coupling assembly that sits on top of the vessel and provides the rotational power. The shaft connects to the drive. When an agitator fails, you need to determine whether the problem is in the drive (motor, gears, bearings) or the shaft (corrosion, bending, impeller damage). If the shaft corroded or bent, you need a shaft/mixer agitator replacement. If the gears or motor failed, you need a [drive agitator](/equipment/agitator/drive/) replacement. IPP stocks both [shaft/mixer agitators](/equipment/agitator/shaft-mixer/) and [drive agitators](/equipment/agitator/drive/).

**Q: Do I need a new shaft or a new drive — how do I tell?**
A: To determine whether you need a shaft or a drive replacement, inspect the following: Check the shaft for visible corrosion pitting, erosion, or surface damage where it contacts the process media. Measure shaft runout — if TIR exceeds 0.10 mm (0 in)/m, the shaft is bent. Inspect the impeller blades for corrosion holes, thinning, or breakage. If any of these conditions exist, you need a shaft/mixer agitator. For the drive side: listen for unusual noise from the gearbox (indicates gear or bearing failure), check for oil leaks at the seal or housing, and verify motor amperage draw. If the drive components are failing, you need a [drive agitator](/equipment/agitator/drive/). In aggressive chemical environments, shaft corrosion is the most common failure mode — especially on reactors using stainless steel shafts in service that demands Hastelloy C-276 or titanium.
