## Spherical vs Conical Dryers

Spherical and conical dryers both dry a batch under vacuum against a heated vessel wall, while vessel geometry and agitator style mark the difference. The table contrasts a spherical dryer with a conical dryer on each factor.

| Factor | Spherical | Conical |
|-----|-----|-----|
| Vessel | A round, ball-shaped vessel | A tapered, cone-shaped vertical vessel |
| Agitator | A curved paddle or blade agitator, often with a high-speed chopper | A helical screw or ribbon that orbits the cone wall |
| Shear | Medium to high shear that breaks up lumps | Low to medium shear with gentle lifting and folding |
| Discharge | A bottom full-port valve with near-complete discharge | A bottom central outlet |

**Q: How does a spherical vacuum dryer work?**
A: A spherical vacuum dryer combines heat, agitation and vacuum. Hot water, steam or thermal oil in the jacket heats the vessel wall, a slow curved agitator sweeps the wall and turns the product, and the vacuum pump lowers the pressure so the moisture or solvent evaporates at a lower temperature. Vapor passes a dust filter to a condenser, and the dry product leaves through a bottom discharge valve.

**Q: Why does the spherical shape matter?**
A: A sphere leaves few dead zones, so the bottom valve achieves near-complete discharge, and the round shape withstands internal pressure well. IMS lists explosion-shock resistant construction on IPP's Rosenmund AG units.

**Q: What is the difference between design and operational capacity?**
A: IMS separates design capacity, the full vessel volume, from operational capacity, the working batch the agitator handles. IPP's Rosenmund AG dryers list 2.4 m³ (83 ft³) of design capacity and the Pierre Guerin Turbosphere 2.8 m³ (98 ft³), with 1.98 m³ (70 ft³) of operational capacity on each.
