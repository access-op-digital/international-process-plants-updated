Peeler vs Pusher Centrifuges

Peeler and pusher centrifuges both separate solids from liquids in a rotating basket through a filter medium, and they differ in how the cycle runs and how the cake leaves the basket. Here is how the two designs compare across key factors, and IPP's peeler vs pusher centrifuge guide covers the differences in more depth.

Factor Peeler Centrifuge Pusher Centrifuge
Operation Automated batch cycles: fill, spin, wash, dry-spin and peel Continuous: feeding, separation, washing and discharge run at the same time
Cake Discharge A peeler knife cuts the cake from the basket wall at peeling speed A reciprocating pusher plate moves the cake along the basket to the discharge edge
Residual Heel A thin heel layer usually stays on the filter cloth between cycles No heel cycle; the cake moves forward continuously
Feed Handles variable feed concentrations and slow-draining solids Needs a consistent, fast-draining, free-flowing feed
Cake Washing Wash steps are set within each batch cycle Wash contact time follows the continuous flow rate

Available Materials of Construction

Peeler centrifuges are specified by basket material, and IPP's inventory covers the basket constructions below.

Stainless Steel

Stainless steel baskets represent the largest share of IPP's peeler centrifuge inventory, in grades SS 316, SS 316L, SS 904 and austenitic stainless steel. Grades 316 and 316L contain molybdenum, which improves pitting resistance compared with grade 304.

Titanium

Titanium baskets in stock come from Luwa-Heine, SMS Buss and Krauss Maffei. Titanium forms a stable, self-healing oxide layer that resists pitting in oxidizing conditions.

Hastelloy & Rubber-Lined

Hastelloy C-22, Hastelloy B and rubber-lined baskets complete the inventory. Hastelloy is a nickel-molybdenum-chromium alloy that resists both oxidizing and reducing media, and a rubber lining protects a steel basket from abrasion and direct chemical attack.

A peeler centrifuge works on the principle of batch filtration in a rotating perforated basket. Slurry fills the basket, centrifugal force drives the liquid through the filter cloth, and the solids build a cake on the basket wall. The cake is washed and spun dry, then the basket slows to peeling speed and a peeler knife cuts the cake away so the solids drop through a discharge chute. A thin residual heel usually stays on the filter cloth for the next cycle, and IPP's step-by-step guide covers each stage in detail.
The two designs differ in the axis of the rotating basket. A horizontal unit turns its basket on a horizontal axis and guides the peeled cake out through a side discharge chute, while a vertical peeler centrifuge turns on a vertical axis and drops the peeled solids through a bottom discharge, which can call for a speed reduction during peeling. Both designs run the same fill, spin, wash, dry-spin and peel cycle, and the Krauss Maffei HZ units in IPP's inventory are horizontal machines.
In a siphon peeler centrifuge, the basket has a solid outer shell and the filtrate drains through axial channels into a siphon chamber at the rear of the basket, where a pivoting skimmer pipe draws it off. The radial distance between the filter cloth and the liquid level in the siphon chamber adds driving force to the filtration. Krauss Maffei designates this design HZ Si, and IPP's inventory includes HZ-125-SI, HZ 125/3.2 SI D, HZ125/2.5Si and HZ 1250/3,2 Si D siphon models.
